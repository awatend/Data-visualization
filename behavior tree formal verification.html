The Ambiguity Gap in Modern Robotics

Behavior Trees (BTs) are a dominant paradigm for programming complex robot actions due to their modularity and flexibility. However, a critical weakness persists: the lack of a standardized, formal definition for their behavior. This "ambiguity gap" means that crucial operational semantics—how tasks run, halt, or handle data—are often left to interpretation. For robots in critical applications, from autonomous drones to surgical assistants, this ambiguity is unacceptable. It prevents rigorous mathematical proof of safety and reliability, creating a fundamental barrier to trust.

What we prove at design time is exactly what runs on the robot, eliminating the gap between specification and implementation.

Offline Analysis: Proving Correctness

Before deployment, the TINA toolbox analyzes the formal model to mathematically prove critical properties. The `sift` tool explores every possible state the system can enter, while the `selt` tool checks these states against safety and liveness rules written in Linear Temporal Logic (LTL).

Verification results from the UAV drone case study demonstrate the ability to formally prove critical safety properties are always met.

Online Execution: Validated Behavior

The HIPPO engine executes the very same formal model on the robot. This ensures the verified behavior is what actually occurs. Visual feedback provides real-time insight into the BT's execution flow, with nodes changing color to reflect their status: Ticked, Running, Success, or Failure.

Case Study: Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV)

An autonomous survey drone's BT was analyzed. The framework successfully verified complex interactions and safety-critical logic, handling a massive state space to provide mathematical guarantees of correct behavior.

Case Study: ROS2 Nav2 Custom Nodes

The framework isn't limited to standard BTs. We extended it to verify custom nodes from the popular ROS2 Nav2 stack, such as the `Recovery` node. This demonstrates the ability to formally prove the correctness of new, domain-specific extensions.

Verifying the `Recovery` Node Logic

The `Recovery` node is designed to retry a primary action if it fails, by executing a secondary recovery action. We formally proved its logic: if the primary action fails, the recovery action is triggered. If the recovery succeeds, the primary action is retried. If the recovery action itself fails, the entire node correctly reports failure.
